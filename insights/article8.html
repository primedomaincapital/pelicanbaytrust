How Long Does It Take to Move a Trust to Florida?

A Delaware trust can be administered from Naples in a matter of weeks. A decanting into a new Florida trust takes a season. A contested move takes a year. The calendar depends on which door the family walks through.
Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The family had lived in Naples for eleven years. Their trust had not. It was a Delaware trust, drafted when Delaware was the obvious choice, administered by a Delaware trustee, governed by Delaware law, and it had served the family well. But the children were in Florida, the grandchildren were in Florida, the family’s accountant and attorney were in Florida, and every distribution, every accounting, and every phone call ran through a trustee a thousand miles away who had never met the beneficiaries. The settlor asked the question every Florida family with an out-of-state trust eventually asks: how long would it take to move the trust here? The answer, the attorney said, was that it depended on which door the family walked through, and the doors had very different calendars.

The trust is not a piece of furniture. It is a legal arrangement governed by the law of the state where it was created, administered by the trustee named in the document, and funded with assets titled in the trust’s name. Moving it means changing one or more of those three things: the governing law, the trustee, or the trust itself. Each change has its own procedure, its own notice period, and its own failure mode, and the family that understands the three doors before choosing one will finish months earlier than the family that discovers them in sequence.

The three ways a trust moves

The first door is the simplest: change the trustee and the place of administration, and leave the trust itself alone. Florida law allows a trustee to transfer the principal place of administration to Florida, with notice to the qualified beneficiaries, and the trust continues under its original governing law. The Delaware trust becomes a Delaware trust administered from Naples, by a Florida trustee, under Florida’s administrative rules. The move takes weeks, not months, because nothing about the trust’s terms changes. The limitation is also plain: the trust is still a Delaware trust. Its duration, its directed-trust provisions, and its governing law are Delaware’s, and a family that wanted Florida’s thousand-year rule of perpetuities or Florida’s directed trust statute has not gotten either.

The second door is substitution of fiduciaries, the procedure Florida added to its trust code for exactly this situation. Under Florida Statutes section 660.46, a trust company or bank can petition the court to substitute itself as trustee, and the substitution takes effect within thirty days of the court’s order, or within ninety days if the order so provides. The procedure is designed for the clean case: the current trustee resigns or is removed, the new trustee is qualified, and the court approves the handover. It is faster than a full decanting and more formal than a simple change of administration, and it is the door most Naples families walk through when the trust’s terms are already sound and only the trustee needs to change.

The third door is decanting: pouring the assets of the old trust into a new trust, drafted under Florida law, with new terms. Florida’s decanting statute, section 736.04117, allows a trustee with discretionary distribution authority to decant, with sixty days’ notice to the qualified beneficiaries, and the notice can be waived. Senate Bill 262, enacted in 2025, amended the statute to clarify the trustee’s authority and the notice procedure. Decanting is the most powerful door, because the new trust can be a Florida trust in full: Florida governing law, Florida’s thousand-year duration, Florida’s directed trust provisions, and any other term the family wants to add. It is also the slowest, because drafting a new trust, giving notice, and retitling the assets takes a season, and because a beneficiary who objects can turn the season into a year.

The calendar, step by step

For the simple move, the calendar is measured in weeks. The new trustee is selected, the notice is sent to the qualified beneficiaries, the resignation and appointment documents are executed, and the assets are retitled or re-registered in the new trustee’s name. The retitling is the step most families underestimate: a trust that holds real estate, closely held business interests, and a dozen brokerage accounts cannot be retitled in a day, and each asset has its own paperwork, its own counterparty, and its own queue. A trust that holds only marketable securities moves in a month. A trust that holds a family business, a partnership interest, and a piece of commercial real estate moves in a quarter.

For a decanting, the calendar is measured in months. The new trust is drafted, which takes four to eight weeks for a complex family trust. The sixty-day notice runs. The assets are transferred, which takes as long as the retitling takes, and the old trust is wound down. The total, for a straightforward decanting, is three to six months. For a contested one, there is no calendar, because a beneficiary’s objection moves the matter to the court, and the court’s calendar is not the family’s calendar.

The tax calendar runs in parallel, and it is the one that catches families by surprise. Moving a trust to Florida does not change its federal tax treatment, and Florida imposes no state income tax on the trust, which is one reason the move is attractive. But a trust that was a Delaware resident trust may have been paying Delaware tax, and a trust that becomes a Florida trust may stop paying it, which is a saving the family should quantify before the move, not discover after it. The federal estate and gift tax exemption, now $15 million per person and permanent under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, means most families’ trusts are no longer taxable at the federal level at all, which removes one of the reasons families used to hesitate about changing a trust’s situs.

What slows the move down

Three things slow a trust move, and none of them is the statute. The first is beneficiary disagreement. A move that changes the trustee, the governing law, or the trust’s terms changes the beneficiaries’ rights, and a beneficiary who believes the change harms them can object, petition the court, and stop the calendar. The sixty-day decanting notice exists precisely so that beneficiaries have time to object, and the objection, when it comes, is usually about control: the new trustee, the new directed-trust provisions, or the new distribution standard. The families that move fastest are the ones that talk to the beneficiaries before the notice goes out, not after.

The second is the assets. A trust that holds a closely held business cannot be retitled without the business’s other owners, its lenders, and its operating agreement, and any of them can slow the transfer. A trust that holds real estate in multiple states needs a deed in each state. A trust that holds a partnership interest needs the partnership’s consent. The retitling is a project, and like every project, it takes as long as its slowest counterparty.

The third is the directed trust. A family that has a directed trust, where one person directs investments and another administers, has two fiduciaries to move instead of one, and the directed-trust provisions of the old state may not match the directed-trust provisions of the new one. Florida’s directed trust statute, in effect since July 2021, is one of the most flexible in the country, and it is often the reason a family chooses Florida in the first place. But the flexibility has to be drafted into the new trust, and the drafting has to be done before the move, not during it.

The case for leaving it where it is

The counterargument deserves a hearing, because it is the argument the Delaware trustee makes, and it is not wrong. Delaware has the deepest bench of trust companies in the country, the most developed trust law, and a court, the Court of Chancery, that decides trust disputes with a speed and expertise no other state matches. South Dakota has a thousand-year duration, no state income tax, and a privacy regime that Florida does not offer. A trust that is well administered in Delaware, by a trustee the family trusts, does not need to move, and the cost of moving it, in fees, in time, and in the risk of a beneficiary dispute, may exceed the benefit of having the trustee down the street.

The counterargument fails on the human calendar, not the legal one. A trust is administered for decades, and the trustee who is a thousand miles away is a trustee the beneficiaries will never meet, whose office they will never visit, and whose judgment they will never see up close. The family that moves its trust to Florida is not buying a better statute. It is buying proximity, and proximity is the thing that makes a trust work: the trustee who knows the family, the property, and the local courts, and who is there when the distribution decision has to be made. The statute is the reason to move. The proximity is the reason the move is worth the months it takes.

How to run the move

The practical version starts with a decision, not a document. Choose the door before drafting anything: a simple change of administration if the trust’s terms are sound, a substitution if the trustee needs to change, a decanting if the terms need to change. Talk to the beneficiaries before the notice, not after, and put the new trustee’s fees in writing before the old trustee resigns. Inventory the assets and their retitling requirements before setting a date, because the date is set by the slowest asset, not the fastest. And remember that the trust’s move is the family’s move: the trust should live where the family lives, under the law the family understands, administered by the trustee the family can reach. A trust that lives a thousand miles from its beneficiaries is a trust that will be administered by correspondence, and correspondence is a poor substitute for a conversation.
