The Insurance Bill That Moved the Market: Florida’s Great Reprieve

Homeowners insurance rates fell in 51 of Florida’s 67 counties in 2026, Citizens cut rates for the first time since 2015, and its policy count fell 84% from its peak. The bill that stalled the Gulf Coast migration is shrinking.
Prime Domain Capital · November 2026 · 4 min read

A Pelican Bay homeowner opened her renewal notice this autumn and did something she had not done in four years: she read the number twice, because it was smaller. The premium had fallen. Not by much, and not enough to erase the tripling of the years before, but the direction had changed, and in a market where the insurance bill had become the second mortgage, direction is the whole story.

The numbers behind her notice are the most important figures in Florida real estate in 2026, and almost nobody outside the insurance world has published them in one place. The Florida Office of Insurance Regulation reported this summer that homeowners insurance rates decreased in 51 of Florida’s 67 counties in 2026. Citizens Property Insurance, the state-created insurer of last resort, recommended a statewide average rate cut of about 2.6% for 2026, its first decrease since 2015, and 44 carriers have filed rate decreases since 2024. Citizens reported 216,577 policies in force as of October 23, 2026, down from a peak of 1,407,805 on September 30, 2023. That is an 84% drawdown: most of those homes have moved back onto private paper.

The level matters as much as the change. The average owner-occupied homeowners policy in force in Florida cost $3,675.31 a year as of May 31, 2026, according to the Office of Insurance Regulation’s Market Intelligence Report, computed from the filings of 95 insurers covering 4,161,048 policies and $15.29 billion of premium. Citizens’ own average owner-occupied premium was $3,216.42, about $459 below the market average. The statewide average rose 31.3% between the second quarter of 2022 and May 2026, from $2,798.24 to $3,675.31, so the reprieve is real but partial: the bill is falling from a peak, not returning to where it was.

What the reprieve did to Naples

The Naples market felt the insurance crisis as a transaction tax. Buyers who could afford a $2 million villa could not always stomach a $30,000 annual premium plus flood coverage, and sellers watched deals die in underwriting rather than in negotiation. The September 2026 figures from the Naples Area Board of REALTORS show what happened when that tax began to lift: 623 closed sales, up 2.5% from a year earlier, an overall median closed price of $575,000, up 4.5%, and inventory down 17.3% to 4,240 properties, or 5.5 months of supply. Single-family homes closed at a median $747,500, up 3.1%, while condos came in at $400,000, down 2.4%. The average days on market improved to 99 from 107. October extended the pattern, with the Naples median closed price reaching $598,500, up 4.1% year over year.

Pelican Bay itself tells the same story at community scale. Worthington Realty’s Pelican Bay figures, updated October 11, 2026, showed a median of $1,297,500 at 94.8% of asking price, with 112 homes listed for sale against 15 under contract. The community’s villas had already shown the way: through the first five months of 2026, twenty-four villas closed at a median $2,225,000, up nearly 30% from $1,712,500 a year earlier, while single-family days on market stretched to 230. The villa buyer, who pays cash and self-insures the risk in practice, never waited for the insurance market. The single-family buyer did. The reprieve is, above all, a single-family story.

The counterargument: the risk did not change

The reprieve deserves its skepticism. The hurricane risk has not changed. What changed is the pricing of it, and the pricing changed largely because of Senate Bill 2-A, the 2022 reform that cut litigation costs: Citizens’ own filing shows the indicated actuarially sound premium for its largest homeowner segment falling 43% between 2024 and 2026, from $6,347 to $3,617, largely attributable to the reform. That is a legislative achievement, not a meteorological one. The next major storm tests whether the new structure holds, and a single landfall can rewrite a year of rate filings.

The reprieve is also uneven in a way that matters for a place brand. Citizens’ county averages run from $836 a year in Baker County to $4,380 in Monroe County, a five-fold spread inside one state. Private-market quotes vary further with roof age, elevation, flood zone, and claims history, so no statewide average describes any specific home. And the 51-of-67 figure counts rate changes, not levels: a county where rates fell 2% from an extreme peak is still expensive. The homeowner who opened her renewal and found a smaller number is real. So is the neighbor whose roof is twenty years old and whose quote went the other way.

There is also the condo problem, which the single-family reprieve does not solve. Post-Surfside reserve requirements and the insurance cost of aging coastal buildings continue to weigh on the condo segment, which is why the Naples condo median fell 2.4% while single-family rose 3.1%. A trust company serving Pelican Bay families needs to understand that split, because the families arriving now are buying houses, not condos, and the families already here are watching their condo assessments.

What it means for the families, and the firms that serve them

Insurance was the hidden tax on the Great Wealth Migration. It did not stop the migration, but it slowed it, selected for cash buyers, and pushed transactions toward the villa product that least needed it. Its reversal removes the last brake. The families who waited out the crisis, renting in Naples while their New York or Chicago home sold, are now the buyers. They arrive with estate plans to restate, trusts to re-site under Florida law, and foundations to fund. They need fiduciaries who understand that the insurance reprieve is a window, not a guarantee, and who can plan for the next storm as well as the last one.

That is the commercial logic of a place brand in this moment. A trust company named for Pelican Bay does not need to explain the community to a prospect who already knows it, and it does not need to explain the insurance reprieve to a family that just lived through it. The name does the qualifying before the first meeting. The market that stalled on a premium notice is moving again, and the firms that serve it will be the ones whose names were already in the room when the renewal came back smaller.
