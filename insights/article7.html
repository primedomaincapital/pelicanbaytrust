Directed Trusts in Florida: When the Family Keeps the Investment Decisions

A Naples founder built a business worth $40 million and trusts his son’s judgment on investments more than any bank’s. He does not want the bank to pick the stocks — he wants it to keep the books, file the returns, and stay out of the way. Florida law, since July 2021, has a name for that arrangement.
Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The founder was seventy-one, his company sold, his children grown, and his estate plan nearly finished — except for one argument. The bank he wanted as trustee ran a fine trust department and a mediocre investment committee, and he knew it, because he had watched its portfolios for a decade. His son, who had run the family’s money since his twenties and beaten the bank’s returns in eight of the last ten years, was the obvious person to manage the investments. But naming his son as trustee meant handing him the checkbook, the tax filings, the accountings, and the legal liability for all of it — and handing his siblings a reason to sue him. The family wanted the bank’s administration and the son’s judgment. For most of Florida’s history, that combination had no clean legal form. Since July 1, 2021, it does.

The form is the directed trust: a trust in which someone other than the trustee holds a power over some part of the trust’s administration, and the trustee must follow that person’s direction. The son directs the investments. The bank administers the trust, keeps the records, files the returns, and makes the distributions the document allows. Neither does the other’s job, and the statute — not a handshake, and not a side letter — says who is liable when something goes wrong.

What changed in July 2021

Florida adopted the Florida Uniform Directed Trust Act in July 2021, adding it as Part XIV of the Florida Trust Code, Sections 736.1401 through 736.1416, under Chapter 2021-183 of the Laws of Florida. The Florida Bar’s Real Property, Probate and Trust Law Section sought the enactment after years in which trust instruments had already been naming trust protectors, trust directors, and trust advisers, while the statute books offered almost nothing about what those people were or what they owed anyone.

The new act starts with definitions, and the definitions do real work. A “directed trust” is a trust whose terms grant a power of direction. A “directed trustee” is a trustee subject to a trust director’s power of direction. A “trust director” is a person granted a power of direction exercisable while not serving as trustee — and the statute adds that a person is a trust director whether or not the trust calls them one, and whether or not they are a beneficiary or the settlor. A son named in the document as “investment adviser” is a trust director under Florida law regardless of the label. By January 2022, sixteen states had adopted the uniform act in some form, Florida among them, according to The Florida Bar Journal.

The act also draws a boundary around itself. Powers of appointment are excluded, as is the power to appoint or remove a trustee or trust director, a settlor’s power over a trust while it is revocable, and certain powers held in a nonfiduciary capacity to achieve the settlor’s tax objectives — including the power to reimburse the settlor for income tax on grantor-trust income. The exclusions matter because an excluded power carries no fiduciary duty under the act unless the trust terms impose one.

The three chairs

A directed trust has three seats, and the confusion starts when two of them are occupied by the same person. The settlor creates the trust and writes the rules. The trust director holds a defined power — usually investment direction, sometimes distribution decisions, sometimes the power to remove and replace the trustee. The directed trustee holds the assets, executes the directions, keeps the books, and deals with the beneficiaries. The director’s power is limited to what the instrument grants.

The director’s seat is the one families misunderstand. Under Section 736.1408, a trust director is subject to the same fiduciary duty and liability as a trustee would have in the same position — the same duty, the same exposure. The trust can modify that duty to the same extent it could modify a trustee’s, but it cannot eliminate the core obligation: the duty to act in good faith and in accordance with the terms and purposes of the trust and the interests of the beneficiaries, which Section 736.0105(2)(b) makes mandatory. A son who directs the trust’s investments is not a helpful volunteer. He is a fiduciary, and his siblings can sue him as one.

The trustee’s seat is narrower than it looks. Under Section 736.1409, a directed trustee must take reasonable action to comply with a direction — and is not liable for doing so, except for its own willful misconduct. The statute does not require the trustee to judge whether the direction is wise: the uniform act’s comments make the point plainly, that the trustee must execute a direction competently, not judge whether it is wise. The one hard limit is willful misconduct: a directed trustee may not comply with a direction if doing so would itself be willful misconduct. Florida, notably, does not define the term — the drafters declined, noting that a definition could spill into other areas of Florida law that use the same phrase without defining it.

And then there is the silence the statute builds in. Under Section 736.1411, neither the trustee nor the director has any duty to monitor the other, or to advise anyone how they might have acted differently. The bank does not have to watch the son’s investment picks. The son does not have to watch the bank’s administration. Each may share information — Section 736.1410 requires them to provide each other information relating to their shared powers, and a director must answer a qualified beneficiary’s written request for information reasonably related to the director’s powers — but neither is the other’s supervisor.

Delaware wrote the first draft

Florida did not invent the directed trust. Delaware did, in substance, with Section 3313 of Title 12 of the Delaware Code, and six other states — Alaska, Nevada, South Dakota, Tennessee, Wyoming, and Michigan — built the early statutory regimes before the Uniform Law Commission promulgated the Uniform Directed Trust Act in July 2017. The uniform act was drafted by a committee chaired by Turney Berry, with reporters John Morley of Yale Law School and Robert Sitkoff of Harvard Law School, and substantial input from the American College of Trust and Estate Counsel.

The difference is the director’s status. Delaware’s statute permits a trust director to serve in a nonfiduciary capacity; Florida’s default is the opposite, a fiduciary like a trustee. A family that wants its director to act freely, without fiduciary exposure, will find Delaware more accommodating. A family that wants its director accountable to the beneficiaries will find Florida’s default more reassuring. Michigan is the outlier that proves the choice is real: it adopted the uniform act in 2019, moving from the older nonfiduciary-permitted model to the fiduciary-default model.

New York, by contrast, has no directed-trust statute at all. A New York trustee who delegates investment decisions keeps the duty to select and monitor the delegate and cannot be fully relieved of liability — which is why, as the Milvidskiy Law Group noted in September 2026, New York families who want this structure often form the trust under another state’s law. Florida’s pitch is geographic as much as legal: under Section 736.1403, Florida is the principal place of administration if the trust terms so provide and a trust director’s principal place of business is in Florida, or a trust director is a Florida resident.

Where it breaks: the disagreement problem

Every directed trust contains the same fault line, and it runs between the two fiduciaries. The director tells the trustee to do something. The trustee doubts it. What happens next decides whether the structure works or becomes a lawsuit with two defendants pointing at each other.

The statute gives the trustee one clean exit: reasonable doubt. Under Section 736.1409(5), a directed trustee that has reasonable doubt about its duty can apply to the court for instructions. That is the pressure valve, and it is also the cost. A trustee that runs to court every time it disagrees with the director will burn the trust’s money on the very disputes the structure was meant to avoid. A trustee that never questions a direction it believes is wrong risks the one thing the statute does not forgive: willful misconduct, a term Florida leaves undefined, to be argued case by case, after the loss has already happened.

The beneficiaries’ position is narrower than most expect. Under the uniform act’s framework, a beneficiary’s main recourse for misconduct by a trust director is an action against the director for breach of the director’s fiduciary duty. The directed trustee answers only for its own willful misconduct. If the director is the settlor’s son, the beneficiary suing the director is suing her brother.

A director who dies, resigns, or becomes incapacitated without a named successor leaves a hole the trustee cannot fill, because the trustee has no power the director held. The act gives the family one tool for the uncertainty: a trustee, settlor, or qualified beneficiary can demand in writing that a director accept or confirm acceptance of the office, and the director must respond within 60 days. None of these are reasons to avoid the structure. All of them are reasons to draft it as if the disagreement has already happened.

The directed trust does not remove the argument about who controls the money. It moves the argument from the signing table, where it is cheap, to the courtroom, where it is not — unless the document answers it first.

The counterargument: sometimes the bank should decide

For all its appeal, the directed trust is not obviously better than the arrangement it replaces, and the families who adopt it for the wrong reason pay for the lesson. The traditional structure — one trustee, holding all the powers and all the liability — has a virtue the split structure lacks: someone is responsible for everything. When a directed trust loses money, the beneficiaries’ first question is who to blame, and the statute’s honest answer is that it depends on which chair made the decision — a question that can take years of litigation to answer.

There is also the fee question nobody puts in the brochure. A directed trust has two fiduciaries, and two fiduciaries charge two fees. The bank’s administration-only schedule is cheaper than its full-service schedule — on a $5 million trust, the difference can run to roughly $19,000 a year — but the director’s fee and the cost of the information-sharing the statute requires are added on top. A family that hires a professional director, such as an investment adviser charging 0.50% to 1% of assets, can end up paying more for the split structure than for the single trustee it replaced.

What it means for the family drafting now

The document is the structure. Five choices in it decide whether a Florida directed trust works, and all five are cheaper to make now than to litigate later. Name the director’s power precisely — investment direction is not distribution direction, and a director who believes they hold both will act on both. Name a successor director. Write the information protocol: what the trustee sends the director, and how often. Write the disagreement procedure: when the trustee doubts a direction, does it ask the director once, in writing, and then comply — or go to court under Section 736.1409(5)? And fix the fees in the document, for both chairs, before either chair has leverage.

Florida built the directed trust for families like the one in Naples: wealthy enough to need a bank, confident enough to keep the investing at home. The statute gives them the split. It does not give them the judgment. That still has to be written, by hand, into the trust — by the settlor, while the settlor is still in the room.
