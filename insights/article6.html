Naples Trust Administration: What Families Pay Their Trustee Each Year

The drafting fee arrives once, as a single invoice. The trustee fee arrives every year, for as long as the trust lasts — and on a $2.2 million Pelican Bay trust it can run past $23,000 a year before a single extra service is billed.
Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The statement arrived in January, as it always did, in the same plain envelope from the same bank. A Pelican Bay couple in their seventies opened it at the kitchen table, past the pages of holdings and transactions, to the one line they had never really read: trustee fee, $23,800. The trust held $2,225,000 in assets, most of it the villa on the bay, valued near the Pelican Bay villa median of $2,225,000 reported by Worthington Realty through the first five months of 2026. Nothing had gone wrong. Nobody had sued anybody. The fee was simply the price of the year, and for the first time they asked what, exactly, the year had cost them — and whether the next thirty years would cost the same.

It is the question almost nobody asks at the signing. Families negotiate the drafting fee, argue over distribution ages, and debate which child should serve as successor trustee. The annual administration fee — the recurring charge deducted from the trust every year it exists — gets a glance, if that. There is no single price. Florida, unlike some states, publishes no fee schedule for trustees. The number comes from the trust document, from the trustee’s own published schedule, or from a court deciding what is reasonable. Three families with identical trusts can pay three different annual bills, and each of them can be correct under the law.

What Florida law actually says

Section 736.0708 of the Florida Statutes is short, and its brevity is the point. If the trust document does not specify the trustee’s compensation, the trustee is entitled to “compensation that is reasonable under the circumstances.” If the document does specify a fee, that figure controls — unless a court finds the duties turned out substantially different from what the settlor contemplated, or the specified amount is unreasonably low or high. A trustee who performs additional services beyond ordinary administration, such as legal work or real estate management, is entitled to separate reasonable compensation for those services on top of the base fee.

Two features of that framework matter more than the words themselves. First, the burden of proof runs the wrong way for a trustee under fire: under Section 736.0206, when fees are challenged, the trustee must prove the compensation is reasonable — the beneficiary does not have to prove it is excessive. Second, Florida has no statutory percentage to argue about. Some states publish tiered commission schedules — 5% on the first $100,000, stepping down through 4%, 3%, and 2.5% before reaching 2% above $5 million, in the pattern LegalClarity described in its 2026 trustee-fee guide. Florida is not one of them. “Reasonable” is the whole standard, argued from the size of the trust, the complexity of the assets, and the work actually performed. That vagueness is why the trustee’s published schedule, agreed in advance, carries so much weight: it is the closest thing to a price list the system provides.

The published schedules

Corporate trustees do publish their prices, and the schedules rhyme even when the numbers differ. The range LegalClarity reported in 2026 runs from roughly 0.5% to 2% of trust assets a year, with the percentage falling as the trust grows — a $1 million trust might pay 1%, about $10,000, while a $10 million trust might pay closer to 0.5%, about $50,000. Most corporate trustees also impose a minimum annual fee, commonly $3,000 to $10,000, which means small trusts pay a disproportionately large share of their value in fees.

Virginia National Bank’s trust and estate fee schedule, effective April 23, 2025, shows how a regional bank prices the work. With investment management included, the bank charges 120 basis points on the first $1 million, 100 on the next $1 million, 80 on the next $3 million, and 40 above $5 million, with a $5,000 minimum annual fee. Without investment management — the trustee administers but does not pick the investments — the schedule drops to 60 basis points on the first $2 million, 50 on the next $3 million, and 40 on the next $5 million, with the same $5,000 minimum. The schedule covers trustee under will, irrevocable trustee under agreement, guardianships, and conservatorships, including delegated or directed trusts.

Vanguard National Trust Company, the low-cost end of the market, requires $500,000 in Vanguard assets to establish a trust relationship. Its long-published investment management schedule charges 0.70% on the first $1 million, 0.35% on the next $1 million, and 0.20% thereafter, with a $4,500 minimum annual fee and a separate $2,500 administration charge per trust registration when Vanguard serves as sole or co-trustee. A 2026 review by FatFire put Vanguard’s trustee fee at roughly 0.25% to 0.50%, against 0.50% to 1.50% at firms such as Northern Trust and Wilmington Trust, which typically require $1 million to $2 million in trust assets at minimum. Fidelity Personal Trust Company, by the same published-schedule summaries, starts trust administration at 0.45% on the first $2 million of assets, with a $4,500 minimum annual fee and a $1 million minimum account size. Members Trust Company publishes minimums of $2,500 a year for revocable living trusts, $3,000 for irrevocable trusts, and $3,500 for special needs trusts.

The arithmetic on three trusts

Take a $5 million trust, the size a Pelican Bay family with a villa and a portfolio reaches without trying. On Virginia National Bank’s 2025 schedule with investment management, the bill is $12,000 on the first million, $10,000 on the second, and $24,000 on the remaining $3 million — $46,000 a year, a blended 0.92%. Without investment management, the same trust costs $12,000 on the first $2 million and $15,000 on the next $3 million — $27,000, or 0.54%. On Vanguard’s schedule, the same $5 million costs $7,000 on the first million, $3,500 on the second, and $6,000 on the remaining $3 million, plus the $2,500 administration charge — $19,000 all in, a blended 0.38%. The gap between the dearest and cheapest published price for the same $5 million trust is $27,000 a year. Over twenty years, before any compounding, that is $540,000 — more than a fifth of the trust itself.

Now the couple at the kitchen table. Their $2,225,000 trust, priced on the Virginia National Bank schedule with investment management, costs $12,000 on the first million, $10,000 on the second, and $1,800 on the remaining $225,000 — $23,800 a year. That is the number on their statement, to the dollar. It is not a mistake, and it is not a scandal. It is a published schedule applied to a published asset value.

The minimum fee is where small trusts get hurt worst. A $500,000 trust at a bank with a $5,000 minimum pays 1% of its value even if the published percentage is 0.5% — the minimum, not the rate, sets the bill. A $250,000 trust at the same minimum pays 2%. This is the arithmetic behind the advice every estate planner in Naples gives and almost no client follows: do not name a corporate trustee for a small trust unless the trust document caps the fee or the trustee waives the minimum in writing.

The bill beyond the basis points

The asset-based fee is the headline, not the total. LegalClarity’s 2026 guide warns that corporate trustees commonly layer separate charges on top of the base fee: real estate management, tax return preparation, account termination, extraordinary services such as litigation support, and sometimes a one-time acceptance fee when the bank takes the trust on. A trust tax return, Form 1041, typically costs $1,000 to $5,000 a year depending on complexity, according to estate-settlement cost data compiled by Real Cost Report. A trust that holds rental property, a family business, or a concentrated stock position can collect surcharges on each.

Then there is the tax bill the trustee’s distribution policy controls. A non-grantor trust that keeps its income pays federal tax under the compressed trust brackets: for 2026, the top 37% rate starts at just $16,000 of taxable income, under IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-32, while a single individual does not reach 37% until taxable income passes $640,600. Income distributed to beneficiaries is generally taxed at their rates instead, which is why a trustee who distributes promptly can save the family more in a year than the trustee’s own fee costs.

The fee is not the scandal. The scandal is that nobody showed the family the arithmetic when the choice of trustee was still reversible at the price of a letter.

The counterargument: the fee buys something real

None of this means the cheapest trustee is the right trustee, and the families who shop on basis points alone usually regret it. A corporate trustee buys continuity no individual can offer: it does not die, divorce, move to Oregon, or fall out with the beneficiaries. It keeps records a court will accept, files the returns on time, and says no to a distribution request without ruining Thanksgiving. The families who learn this the expensive way are the ones who named a well-meaning child as trustee and watched the siblings spend years — and a multiple of the distribution’s value — in legal fees arguing over it.

The dearest schedule also buys a different kind of insurance. When a beneficiary challenges a distribution, or a creditor comes after trust assets, or the IRS questions a valuation, the trustee defends the trust with its own lawyers and its own balance sheet behind it. Vanguard’s trust services, by contrast, are limited to liquid, publicly traded holdings; a full-service bank trustee will hold the villa, manage the rental houses, and sit in a Collier County courtroom over a boundary dispute, and the 120 basis points on the first million is partly the price of that willingness. A directed trust, where the family keeps investment decisions and hires the bank only to administer, can cut the bill nearly in half — $27,000 against $46,000 on the $5 million example — but only if the family actually has someone competent to direct.

There is also a limit to the shopping. The largest trust companies do not compete for small trusts at any price, and the cheapest ones do not take the assets that make a Pelican Bay trust complicated: the villa, the closely held business, the art, the mineral interests. A family that optimizes for the lowest basis points and then discovers its trustee cannot hold the family’s actual assets has saved money on a service it cannot use.

What it means for the family choosing now

Three questions decide whether a trustee fee is fair, and all three should be asked before the trust is signed, when the answer still costs nothing. First, get the schedule in writing — the full schedule, with the minimum, the tiers, and the surcharges for real estate, tax preparation, and termination. A trustee that will not put its price in writing has told you its price. Second, ask who pays when the assets are illiquid. A trust that is mostly a villa cannot pay a $23,800 fee from a villa; it pays from the portfolio, or it borrows, or it sells — and the document should say which. Third, ask what happens when the family wants out. Some trustees charge a termination fee; some make resignation slow; the trust document can fix both in advance, and almost never does.

The deeper point is about time. A trust is the longest financial commitment a family ever makes — Florida law allows one to run for centuries — and its annual fee compounds like any other cost. A $27,000-a-year difference, repeated for thirty years, is $810,000 before interest, which is a second villa. Families who would spend a month negotiating the price of a car will sign a trust document in an afternoon without asking what the trustee charges. The statement arrives every January regardless.
