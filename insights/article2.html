The Families Behind the Gates: Naples' Quiet Capital Inflow

Who is moving to Southwest Florida, what they hold, and why their trustees are following them south.
Prime Domain Capital · October 2026 · 7 min read

The moving trucks tell one story; the trust filings tell another. Every year, IRS migration data confirms what anyone driving down Tamiami Trail already senses: adjusted gross income is leaving New York, New Jersey, Illinois, and California and arriving in Florida at historic scale. Between 2020 and 2023, Florida's net AGI gain from domestic migration ran into the tens of billions — the largest of any state, by a wide margin.

But the families themselves are quieter than the statistics. They arrive without announcement, buy behind gates, and bring with them something more consequential than their spending: their balance sheets, their foundations, and their estate plans.

The profile of the arriving family

Demographers who study the inflow describe a consistent composite. The typical relocating household is led by someone in their late fifties to early seventies, with investable assets in the $10–50 million range and often a family business already sold or in transition. Many maintain a northern residence for part of the year — but Florida, increasingly, is where the domicile, the doctors, the grandchildren's school visits, and the trust situs all converge.

Collier County consistently ranks among the top U.S. counties for per-capita income. In Pelican Bay specifically, the housing stock — much of it held in trust or LLC structures — turns over at price points that imply eight- and nine-figure household balance sheets as a matter of routine.

“The wealth arriving in Naples doesn't announce itself. It files a change of domicile and hires a trustee.”

Why trustees follow the money

When a family relocates, its fiduciary relationships face a choice: administer from afar, or establish Florida presence. The economics favor presence. Trust administration is a relationship business — annual reviews, distribution decisions, beneficiary education — and families prefer those conversations across a table, not across time zones.

The result is a steady institutional migration shadowing the personal one. National trust companies have expanded their Naples footprints for a decade; regional players from the Carolinas and Texas have opened offices; and a handful of purpose-built Florida trust companies now compete directly with century-old northern institutions for the same families.

The local-name advantage

In a market this relationship-driven, the name on the door carries unusual weight. A family choosing between a national brand's Naples office and a firm named for the community itself is making a statement about belonging. Pelican Bay residents, in particular, chose a private world with its own beach, tram, and governance — they respond to institutions that signal insider status rather than branch-office presence.

The capital inflow shows no sign of slowing. The baby-boom cohort — the wealthiest generation in American history — is still early in its relocation wave, and state tax differentials remain the original accelerant. For fiduciaries, the question is not whether the demand arrives. It is whether the name above the door is the one these families already trust.
